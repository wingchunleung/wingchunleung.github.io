    Why we named it Shepherd 🐑

    A shepherd guides and protects its flock. Shepherd does the same for users -- filtering misinformation and surfacing timely guidance in everyday life.

    Team
    Brainstorming

    1. In brainstorming, my team and I focused on things and features around us that we hope to improve. This reminds me of my time at Costco (where I worked in its headquarters in the U.S., as my first full-time job out of college). At Costco, I always have lunch with my colleagues, and one of them always uses this app to track his calories by taking a picture before eating. However, I always wonder: after tracking it, so what? There is no insight -- it seems something is missing.

    2. Furthermore, misinformation and fake news are everywhere after COVID. This is also supported by numerous articles and research. With that, manually verifying the information is time-consuming and increases cognitive load, so my team was thinking: what if we could build something that automatically checks whether information is credible?

    3. Lastly, after COVID, it became clear that people care much more about their personal health (which is also supported by many studies and articles). Because of this, I conducted some quick research on smartwatch adoption rates -- in just the first 6 years of adoption, smartwatches reached around 2.81 times the shipment volume of smartphones during their early adoption period. This suggests a strong public interest in personal health and self-monitoring.

      At the same time, we now have many passive-tracking apps that continuously monitor health data. However, most of these apps still fail to provide truly actionable insights. This shows a gap between what users currently have and what they actually want. Therefore, my team decided to move in this direction and build Shepherd.

    Ideation

    1. In terms of ideation, I believe the product we ultimately want to build can already be partially mimicked by some existing tools, so my team analyzed this using the GOMS framework (Goals, Operators, Methods, Selection Rules) -- to map what a user does today versus what they should have to do. The left column shows the current path, which is time-consuming, and our goal is to skip all the intermediate steps. So the assistant, (i.e., Shepherd), will automatically dive into your data and then give you actual insights, while also flagging and identifying fake information and misinformation.

    2. From there, we also created sketches (low-fidelity drawings) to explain what we are trying to build.

      The left sketch shows a fake-news notification system. When the system detects fake news while you are scrolling through social media, it will automatically send a notification flagging the content.

      On the right are the actionable insights. For example, while you are scrolling through your calorie app, the system will automatically dive into your records and personal preferences, then come back with a suggestion for your next meal.

    Prototype 1

    With the above ideations, my team and I started implementing and completed the first prototype.

    User feedback (Prototype 1)

    After implementing the 1st prototype, we interviewed 5 potential users (our friends and family members) using a short questionnaire. The average score was lower than we expected, and one of the interviewees flagged the issue of "too many notifications."

    Prototype 2

    With the user feedback from our 1st prototype, my team and I discussed how to resolve the users' comments and create a product that users truly love. We later agreed on an invisible computing approach (an HCI principle) -- instead of sending notifications, the system would immediately flag content and provide actionable insights on the same screen while users scroll through their phones.

    User feedback (Prototype 2)

    With the invisible computing approach, we conducted a 2nd interview with the same 5 users using another questionnaire (more detailed). Ultimately, the approach received an almost perfect score, showing that the users were satisfied.

    Reflection

    I believe this project is very valuable because it provided me with hands-on experience in implementing products using HCI principles. Not only was I able to practically apply HCI principles in product development, but I also gained hands-on experience in building a product through needfinding, ideation, prototyping, and more. Before taking this course, I always thought building a product meant building everything from start to end in one take. However, this course taught me the right approach -- "build small, verify carefully." Also, despite all team members leveraging the same set of HCI principles when discussing product ideas, we still had different perspectives on how to address users' needs. Overall, the team project experience was enjoyable, and I learned a lot from both the course and the project.

    The Good, the Bad, the Trade-off

    Open in new tab

    Kimi Claw is a 24/7 AI assistant released in February 2026. This application is built on top of OpenClaw, with full integration into Kimi’s ecosystem (using Kimi’s AI model and cloud infrastructure). Kimi Claw aims to automate complex tasks and improve users’ efficiency. However, despite its capabilities, it comes with pros and cons. Due to time constraints, the video focuses on the user desirability aspect.

    In terms of pros, there are 3 main advantages. First, it offers strong automation. Users can input natural language commands, which Kimi Claw automatically translates into executable code. This eliminates the need for manual installation and implementation. Second, it is easy to set up. I recall when I set up my self-hosted OpenClaw on a virtual private server (VPS), it took me a day just to get it functional. However, Kimi Claw allows users to deploy the AI agent with just a few clicks, saving time and reducing cognitive load. Third, it has low maintenance. As OpenClaw has frequent updates, manually tracking and updating can be time-consuming. At Kimi Claw, updates are handled by the Kimi platform, and users can upgrade with a one click.

    Despite the above pros, there are 2 disadvantages. First, Kimi Claw violates the invisible computing principle. Unlike the original OpenClaw, Kimi Claw shows all the reasoning for each operation. While this allows users to review its current status, it becomes overwhelming over time (e.g., dozens of messages for a single operation). Second is the over-automation issue. Unlike traditional coding, where code is visible, changes using Kimi Code occur in a black box, making it unclear what is modified or whether new issues have been introduced.

    With that, we can see that, despite its strengths, Kimi Claw presents a trade-off -- convenience at the expense of reduced transparency and control.

HCI principles I leveraged

Usability Heuristics

  • Consistency Internal, external, and metaphorical consistency across the interface.
  • Mapping Match between system and real world — use language users understand.
  • Minimalism Every extra element competes with relevant information.
  • Freedom User control — undo, redo, emergency exits.
  • Flexibility Accelerators for experts, support for novices.
  • Recognition Make options visible rather than requiring recall.
  • Visibility Keep users informed about system status.
  • Error Prevention Prevent errors before they occur through constraints.
  • Error Recovery Help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors.
  • Help Provide help and documentation when needed.

Gestalt Principles

  • Proximity Close elements are perceived as related groups.
  • Similarity Similar elements are perceived as belonging together.
  • Continuation Elements on a line or curve are seen as related.
  • Closure The mind completes incomplete shapes.
  • Figure-Ground Users distinguish foreground from background.
  • Common Fate Elements moving together are grouped.
  • Symmetry Balanced elements are perceived as unified.

Design Levels

  • Visceral Immediate emotional reaction — sensation, first impression.
  • Behavioral Usability and function — does it work well?
  • Reflective Meaning and self-image — what does it say about me?
